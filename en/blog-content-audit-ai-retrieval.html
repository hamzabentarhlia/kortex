The B2B content audit framework
for AI retrieval readiness.

Most B2B content archives are retrieval graveyards: hundreds of posts that AI models bypass — not because the content is bad, but because it was built for an era when search engines read keywords, not entities. This is the complete diagnostic and rebuild framework, with a scorecard and a checklist you can use today.

By the KORTEX team · Updated October 15, 2026 · 22 min read

Short answer

A GEO content audit (an AI content audit) scores every existing page from 0 to 10 on five criteria answer engines reward — answer-first extractability, entity clarity, structured answer blocks, schema markup and internal linking — then sorts each page into keep (8–10), retrofit (4–7) or retire/rebuild (0–3), and lists the missing pillar, contrarian, FAQ and comparison assets.

Key takeaways
  • Judge pages on whether they strengthen or weaken your entity in AI answers, not on traffic alone.
  • Five criteria, 0–2 points each: answer-first opening, consistent entity terminology, structured answer blocks, schema markup, internal links with descriptive anchors.
  • Retrofit the 4–7 pages first, in traffic order. The single highest-leverage fix is rewriting the opening paragraph as a direct, quotable answer.
  • Fill gaps in this order: missing pillar, high-traffic retrofits, FAQ and comparison gaps, then the long tail.
  • Consolidate thin, overlapping posts into one strong page with 301 redirects; a smaller, coherent archive is easier for models to attribute.

Why are most B2B content archives invisible to AI?

Most archives are invisible to AI because they were written to rank for keywords and to perform on social feeds, not to be extracted and attributed. Their claims are buried, their terminology shifts from post to post, and nothing tells a machine which company owns which idea.

Every B2B company with more than two years of content production has the same problem. They have an archive. Articles from 2021 on "top content marketing trends." Long-form guides on "how to build a B2B content strategy." Case studies written for a sales cycle that has since changed. Product posts for features that were rebranded. All of it indexed, all of it costing crawl budget, all of it contributing noise to the picture that AI models — the ones their buyers now use to discover solutions — form of the company.

The traditional content audit response is to update the pages with traffic and delete the ones without. That approach optimizes for the wrong metric. In AI retrieval, the question is not whether a page has traffic — it is whether the page contributes to or degrades the entity graph that determines citation authority. A low-traffic FAQ page with clean FAQPage schema and consistent entity terminology can be doing real work for AI citation while appearing irrelevant in a traffic-based audit. A high-traffic "ultimate guide" with no structured answer blocks, no entity declarations, and generic terminology can be diluting the signal of the pages around it.

The diagnostic question for every content page is not "does this page get traffic?" It is "does this page strengthen or weaken the entity graph that determines whether AI models cite us?" Those are different questions, and they produce different audit decisions.

How is a GEO content audit different from an SEO content audit?

An SEO audit asks whether a page can rank and earn clicks; a GEO audit asks whether an answer engine can extract a clean claim from the page and attribute it to your company. The two overlap on technical health and internal linking, but they score different things.

What are the five GEO readiness criteria?

The five criteria are answer-first extractability, entity clarity, structured answer blocks, schema markup and internal link architecture. Each is scored 0, 1 or 2, giving a GEO readiness score out of 10 for every page.

Before scoring any individual page, fix these five criteria as the yardstick for every page. They map to what answer engines need in order to use a page: a claim they can lift, an entity they can attach it to, a structure they can parse, a machine-readable declaration of what the page is, and links that show how it fits with the rest of your content.

Criterion 1: answer-first (BLUF) extractability (0–2 points)

Does the page state its core claim in the first 100–150 words, as a complete sentence that could be quoted with attribution? Score 2 if the opening paragraph contains a precise, extractable claim that includes an entity name or mechanism name. Score 1 if the claim appears somewhere in the first section but not in the opening paragraph. Score 0 if the page opens with background context, rhetorical questions, or scene-setting prose before stating any claim.

This is the criterion most existing B2B content fails on, and the one with the highest retrofit leverage. A single paragraph rewrite — replacing the scene-setting opening with a direct claim statement — moves a page from 0 to 2 on this criterion without changing anything else. The rewrite takes minutes.

Before · score 0"In today's fast-moving digital landscape, content has never been more important. But with so much noise out there, how can B2B brands stand out? Let's explore."
After · score 2"Founder-led content operations turn one founder video into an interlinked set of articles, FAQs and comparison pages, so that answer engines can quote the founder's thinking with attribution."

Criterion 2: entity clarity and terminology consistency (0–2 points)

Does the page use the entity's canonical terminology — company name, category label, mechanism name, audience qualifier — consistently and correctly? Score 2 if the entity name and mechanism name appear in their canonical forms in the first 200 words and at least three more times throughout. Score 1 if the terminology is present but inconsistent — synonyms used interchangeably with canonical terms, or the mechanism name absent from the opening. Score 0 if the page could have been written by any company in the space — no entity-specific terminology, no mechanism name, no category label.

To check quickly, keep your terminology canon open next to the page and search the page for each canonical term and its known synonyms. If you do not yet have a canon, building one is the first deliverable of the audit; our category creation playbook shows how.

Criterion 3: structured answer blocks (0–2 points)

Does the page contain structured extraction points — FAQ sections, comparison tables, definition blocks, numbered steps under clear headings — that give AI models clean answer units without parsing long prose? Score 2 if the page contains at least one FAQ section with direct-answer formatting, a comparison table with labeled headers, or a definition block that states "X is Y" explicitly. Score 1 if the page has some structure (numbered lists, sub-headings) but no direct-answer blocks. Score 0 if the page is unbroken narrative prose.

Question-shaped headings ("How does X work?") followed by a one- or two-sentence answer count toward a 2: they mirror the way buyers phrase prompts to answer engines.

Criterion 4: schema markup implementation (0–2 points)

Does the page have machine-readable schema that declares its content type and entity? Score 2 if the page has Article or BlogPosting schema with a clear, answer-first description, an author or publisher pointing to your Organization, plus FAQPage schema if the page contains FAQ content that matches the visible text. Score 1 if the page has basic Article schema but the description is empty or keyword-stuffed. Score 0 if the page has no schema beyond whatever the CMS inserts by default.

A note on expectations: Google has limited FAQ rich results in its own results pages to a narrow set of sites, so FAQPage markup is no longer about getting an enhanced snippet there. Its value in a GEO audit is that it states, in a structured way, which question each answer belongs to — useful to any system parsing the page. Only mark up questions and answers that are visible on the page.

Criterion 5: internal link architecture (0–2 points)

Does the page have three to five internal links using descriptive anchor text that reflects entity terminology, and does it connect to and from the relevant pillar pages? Score 2 if the page links to the primary pillar using the mechanism name as anchor text, links to at least two other related pages with descriptive anchors, and is itself linked from the pillar. Score 1 if internal links exist but use generic anchors ("read more", "click here"). Score 0 if the page has no internal links or is not linked from any related page (an orphan).

The underlying logic — pillars as entity homes, supporting pages as spokes — is laid out in entity-first content architecture.

How do you run a GEO content audit, step by step?

Run it in five steps: build the inventory, score every page, sort pages into three groups, execute retrofits in priority order, and re-test the target questions in answer engines. For 50 pages, expect roughly a working day of scoring and planning before any rewriting starts.

Step 1: build the content inventory

Export a complete list of every indexed content page from your CMS or Google Search Console. Collect, for each page, the information below. If the archive is larger than 200 pages, start with the top 50 by traffic — they have the most to gain from an immediate retrofit and the most to lose from continued underperformance.

Step 2: score each page against the five criteria

For each page, open it, read the first 200 words, skim the structure, check the schema with a validator such as Google's Rich Results Test or the Schema.org validator, and score it against each criterion. Add the five scores to get a total out of 10. Once the criteria are internalized, scoring takes three to four minutes per page — a 50-page audit is roughly three to four hours of focused work.

While scoring, add a one-sentence "failure note" for any criterion below 2 — "opening paragraph is scene-setting, no claim stated", "mechanism name absent from first 200 words", "no link to pillar". These notes become the retrofit instructions for the next step.

Step 3: sort pages into three groups

  • GEO-ready (8–10): already strong. Put them on a quarterly maintenance schedule — keep the FAQ current, confirm the schema still validates, and check that internal links point to current pages. No immediate action beyond monitoring.
  • Retrofit (4–7): solid information on relevant territory, with structural gaps you can fix without a full rewrite. These are the highest-priority pages of the audit: they already have content value and a clear improvement path from the failure notes. Within this group, work in descending traffic order.
  • Retire or rebuild (0–3): off-topic, factually outdated, or so structurally weak that a retrofit would amount to a rewrite — at which point rebuilding from your entity structure document is faster. When retiring, 301-redirect the URL to the most relevant remaining page rather than leaving a dead link.

Step 4: execute retrofits in priority order

Work through the retrofit pages following their failure notes. Common retrofits and typical time per page:

  • Answer-first opening rewrite: 10–20 minutes. Replace the opening paragraph with a direct claim that includes the entity name, mechanism name and core assertion. This often fixes criteria 1 and 2 at once.
  • FAQ section: 30–60 minutes. Identify the three to five questions the page implicitly answers, add an explicit FAQ after the main content, and mark it up with FAQPage schema that matches the visible text.
  • Terminology standardization: 20–40 minutes. Replace synonyms of mechanism names and category labels with canonical terms; replace vague phrases ("our approach", "this method") with the named mechanism.
  • Schema: 15–30 minutes. Add or update Article schema with an answer-first description and a publisher pointing to your Organization; add FAQPage where relevant; confirm site-level Organization schema exists.
  • Internal links: 15–30 minutes. Link to the pillar with the mechanism name as anchor; add two or three related links with descriptive anchors; make sure the pillar links back.

A full retrofit of one page usually takes 90 minutes to two hours when all five elements need work, much less when only one or two do. Working through a prioritized list, a focused person can typically complete several retrofits a day.

Step 5: re-test in answer engines

Before the audit, write down the 15–20 questions your buyers ask when they are looking for what you do. Run them through ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google AI Overviews before the retrofits and again each month after. Log whether answers reuse your framing, use your terminology, or cite your pages by name. This closes the loop: the audit is not finished when pages are edited, but when you can see whether answer engines treat them differently. Our answer engine visibility guide covers how to set up that monitoring.

How do you find pillar gaps and missing entity assets?

Map your pages against four asset types — pillar, contrarian piece, FAQ cluster, comparison page — for each topic you want to own. Any type that is missing is a gap that answer engines are currently filling with a competitor's content or a generic synthesis.

The audit is not only about improving existing pages. It is also about spotting structural gaps that no existing page covers. These gaps are often worth more than any single retrofit, because they correspond to questions AI models are already answering without you.

The four gap types

Missing pillar gap: the topic has supporting content (FAQ clusters, comparison posts, use-case descriptions) but no primary pillar article that defines the thesis, mechanism and category with precision. This is the most common structural gap in B2B archives: teams produce supporting content before establishing the anchor. The fix is building the pillar first and retrofitting supporting content to link to it.

Missing contrarian gap: there is a pillar and supporting content but no piece that names and rejects the dominant false belief in the category. Without it, your position is defined only by what you claim, not by what you reject — weaker differentiation, and nothing to surface when buyers ask questions like "why isn't X working for us?" or "what is better than X?".

Missing FAQ gap: pillar-level content exists but no structured FAQ cluster. Answer engines that retrieve live web pages work well with self-contained question-and-answer blocks, and an FAQ cluster that maps the 10–15 follow-up questions raised by the pillar gives them exactly that. Of all new assets, it is usually the quickest to produce from material you already have.

Missing comparison gap: pillar and FAQ content exist but no structured page that compares your mechanism with the standard alternative. Comparison questions come from buyers in evaluation mode, which is where being the cited source matters most commercially: a buyer who has just read a comparison sourced from your page arrives at the first call already familiar with your positioning.

Prioritizing new content vs. retrofits

  1. Fill pillar gaps first. Without the anchor page, neither retrofits nor new FAQs have an entity home to link back to.
  2. Retrofit high-traffic pages. They already have an audience; improving them amplifies existing performance.
  3. Fill FAQ and comparison gaps. They capture the follow-up and evaluation questions.
  4. Retrofit the long tail. Lower-traffic pages in descending order, in batches.

What content should you retire, consolidate or keep?

Retire content that is factually outdated, off-topic or too thin to carry signal; consolidate overlapping pages that each answer half a question; keep and retrofit anything relevant, even with low traffic. Always redirect what you remove.

One of the most counterintuitive conclusions of a GEO-focused audit is that some content should be removed entirely — not because it is bad, but because it dilutes the coherence of your entity. Understanding the retire decision prevents the common mistake of preserving every page on the principle that "more is better."

When to retire content

Retire content when it meets any of three conditions: it is factually outdated to the point of creating false impressions of what your company does or claims; it covers territory entirely outside your entity and creates category confusion; or it is so thin — a few hundred words with no structure, no internal links and no entity terminology — that it adds more noise than signal.

The noise concern is real and underappreciated. A model encountering fifty thin, generic posts alongside five well-structured entity-specific pieces sees a company whose signal is mostly generic. A smaller, denser, more coherent body of content is easier to attribute than a large, diffuse one. Sometimes the right move is to consolidate rather than retire.

The consolidation decision

Consolidation is the preferred approach for pages that individually score 2–3 but cover relevant territory. Two posts that each cover half of a question can be merged into one page that answers the full question — with an answer-first opening, an FAQ section, entity terminology, schema and internal links. The merged page becomes the canonical URL; the originals 301-redirect to it. The result is denser and more authoritative than either original, without losing their informational value.

Consolidation is particularly valuable for "series" content — part 1 of 3, part A of a multi-part guide — that was published in installments and never merged. These series are weak citation candidates individually and strong ones once combined, because the combined depth supports criterion 3 and usually covers enough questions to justify an FAQ.

How do you keep content GEO-ready after the audit?

Keep it ready with a three-speed cadence: a monthly check of buyer questions and FAQs, a quarterly score of everything newly published, and a half-yearly review of the whole entity map. Then build the five criteria into your publishing checklist so new pages arrive ready.

Monthly: FAQ freshness check

Run your 20–30 monitoring questions across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google AI Overviews. Note any questions about your category that appear in answers but are not covered by your FAQ clusters, and add them — with direct-answer formatting and matching FAQPage schema.

Quarterly: verify new content

Score every piece published in the previous quarter within 30 days of publication. Flag any page below 6 for immediate retrofit, before it accumulates traffic and becomes harder to change. Better still, make the five criteria part of the publishing checklist so the score is checked before a page goes live.

Every six months: full entity review

Map all pages against their entity relationships, look for new pillar gaps created by category evolution or competitor moves, confirm the terminology canon is still current, and review internal links for broken links, redirect chains and outdated anchors. For an archive of 50–100 pages this usually takes about a day and sets content priorities for the next two quarters.

What does an audit look like in practice?

In practice an audit produces three things: a scored spreadsheet, a prioritized retrofit list, and a short list of missing assets to build next.

Illustrative scenario — not a client result

A B2B software company has 60 blog posts written over four years by different freelancers. The team scores the 40 posts that receive any search traffic.

  • A handful score 8 or more: recent, structured how-to guides. They go on the quarterly maintenance list.
  • Most land between 4 and 7. Typical failure notes: "opens with industry context", "calls the product's method three different names", "no link to the main guide". They get opening rewrites, terminology clean-up and an FAQ, in traffic order.
  • A three-part series on onboarding scores 2–3 per part. It is merged into one guide and the three old URLs redirect to it.
  • Posts about a discontinued feature are retired and redirected.
  • The gap map shows no pillar defining the company's core method and no comparison page against spreadsheets, the alternative most prospects use. Both go to the top of the production plan.

The scenario illustrates the process and the decisions; it does not describe a measured outcome.

Frequently asked questions about GEO content audits

What is a content audit for AI retrieval readiness?

It is a systematic review of every existing page against what AI answer engines need to select and cite a source. Each page is scored on five criteria: answer-first extractability, entity clarity, structured answer blocks, schema markup and internal linking. Pages are then sorted into three groups: GEO-ready, retrofit, and retire or rebuild.

What is the first step in a GEO content audit?

Build the inventory: list every content page with its URL, title, publication date, organic traffic and word count. This spreadsheet becomes the working document for the whole audit. Then prioritize by traffic, because pages that already have visitors gain the most from a retrofit. If you have no terminology canon yet, write it before scoring.

Can a GEO readiness audit be done in one afternoon?

For an archive of 20–30 pages, yes. At three to four minutes per page, scoring takes two to three hours, and sorting and picking the top five retrofits fits in the same afternoon. The retrofits themselves take longer, up to one or two hours per page when all five criteria need work. For 100+ pages, start with the top 30 by traffic and audit the rest in batches.

How long does a GEO content audit take for 50–100 pages?

Plan for roughly 8 to 12 hours of focused work: 2–3 hours to build the inventory and pull traffic data, 4–6 hours to score pages against the five criteria, and 2–3 hours to sort, prioritize and write the action plan. Time scales roughly with the number of pages. Archives of 200+ pages are best sampled: the top 50 by traffic first, then the rest in later phases.

Should we audit before or after creating new content?

Audit first. New content produced before the audit risks contradicting existing pages, because the terminology canon has not been checked against what is already published. The audit shows the current state, identifies the gaps new content should fill and sets the vocabulary it must use. A few days of auditing up front prevents weeks of rework later.

What tools do you need for a GEO content audit?

A spreadsheet, Google Search Console for traffic and position data, and a schema validator such as Google's Rich Results Test or the Schema.org validator. No proprietary tool is required. The audit is mainly a judgment exercise, reading each page against the five criteria; the spreadsheet organizes the judgments and the traffic data sets the order of work.

What is the difference between retrofitting and retiring a page?

Retrofit pages that cover relevant topics but were structured for another era: good information, wrong format. Rewrite the opening as a direct answer, add an FAQ, standardize terminology, add descriptive internal links and schema. Retire pages that are off-topic, factually outdated or so thin that a retrofit would be worse than removing them and redirecting the URL to a stronger page.

Should low-traffic pages be deleted to improve GEO authority?

Not on traffic alone. Decide on relevance to your entity and on retrofit potential. A low-traffic FAQ page on a relevant question, well structured and marked up, can support the pages around it even if it gets few clicks. Delete pages that are off-topic, factually wrong or so thin they add noise; keep and retrofit pages that answer relevant questions.

What is the single most important change in a GEO retrofit?

Rewrite the opening paragraph of every retrofit page as a direct, quotable answer that names the entity and the mechanism. It fixes the most commonly failed criterion across the most pages in the least time: a model that finds a precise, attributable claim at the top of a page does not have to dig it out of the middle of a long article.

How many existing pages are usually GEO-ready without changes?

It depends heavily on how and when the archive was written, so we do not quote a benchmark. In practice, content written for keyword rankings or social engagement tends to fail at least two criteria, most often the answer-first opening and terminology consistency. That is why most archives have real retrofit potential, and why scoring is worth doing before producing anything new.
