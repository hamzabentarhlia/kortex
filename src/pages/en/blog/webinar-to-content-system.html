Key takeaways
  • The raw material is the speaker's first-hand material: their examples, their reasoning and the questions the audience asked.
  • Each asset has to answer a different question. If two assets answer the same one, merge them.
  • AI helps with transcription, structure and first drafts. A person checks every figure, name and claim against the recording.
  • Google warns that generating many pages without adding value for users can violate its scaled content abuse policy. A hard cap on assets is the safeguard.
  • Counting assets tells you what you made. Tracking citations tells you whether it worked.

Why a webinar is good raw material

A webinar contains three things that a blank page does not. First, a speaker who has done the work and explains it in their own words. Second, examples that were not invented for the occasion. Third, a live Q&A, which is a list of questions real people in your market chose to ask.

That matches what Google asks for in its guide to generative AI features: non-commodity content, with a unique point of view and in-depth experience, rather than a restatement of what is already online. A webinar is not automatically that. But it starts closer to it than a brief written from keyword research.

Step 1: Choose the right webinar

Not every recording is worth the effort. Pick one where the speaker took a position, where there are specific cases or numbers that belong to the company, and where the Q&A has questions that go beyond "can you share the slides". A product demo with no argument gives you product pages. A talk where the speaker says why a common approach fails gives you a month of material.

If the source has one idea, the output will be a few strong pages. Do not stretch it.

Step 2: Transcribe

We use speech-to-text on the recording and keep the time codes. Whisper is the model we use for this. The transcript is a working file, not a deliverable. Speech-to-text makes mistakes on names, product terms and numbers, so the transcript is corrected by a person against the audio wherever a name or a figure appears.

Step 3: Extract the argument

Before any writing, we pull six things out of the transcript and put them in a one-page sheet:

  • The core thesis, in one sentence.
  • The named mechanism: how the speaker says it works.
  • The belief the speaker rejects.
  • The examples they return to.
  • The questions the audience asked.
  • The objections the speaker answered.

The same sheet holds the terms to use: company name, product names, the names of any frameworks. Every later asset uses it. This step is where most of the quality is decided, because everything downstream reads from it.

Step 4: Plan assets, with one rule

List the assets the source can support. For each, write the question it answers and the minute of the recording it draws on. A planning table looks like this:

The table is an example, not client work. The rule it shows is the important part: each row answers a different question. If two rows answer the same one, they are one asset. If an asset has no source minute, it is not in the plan.

This rule is a quality gate and a compliance gate. Google's spam policies define scaled content abuse as many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings and not to help users, and give using generative AI tools to produce many pages without adding value as an example. Google's guide also says creating separate content for every variation of a query is ineffective as a long-term strategy. A plan where each asset has its own question and its own source minute is the opposite of that.

Step 5: Draft with AI, in the speaker's terms

With the extraction sheet and the asset plan in hand, we draft each piece with AI assistance and the sheet loaded as the reference. The draft stays close to what the speaker said and shows where the claim came from. The goal is not volume. It is to get a clean first version quickly, so that the human time goes into checking and improving.

We tell readers when it helps. Google suggests giving context about how content was created, including how automation was used, where it makes sense for the audience.

Step 6: Check every fact, with a person

Google's guidance on generative AI content is blunt: generative models predict likely sequences of words and do not retrieve facts, so output may contain inaccuracies, and it is critical to fact-check and review the content manually before publishing. That includes titles, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text.

Our checklist for each piece:

  1. Every figure matches the recording or a cited primary source. If it does not, the figure is cut.
  2. Every name, title and company is spelled the way the speaker spells it.
  3. Every quote is checked against the audio.
  4. Claims the speaker did not make are removed, even when they sound plausible.
  5. The speaker or the client reviews the final text before it goes live.
  6. The title, description and markup describe what the page actually says.

This step cannot be automated. A tool can flag a mismatch. A person decides what is true.

Step 7: Publish with links

Publish the pillar and the FAQ first, because other assets point to them. Link every supporting page to the pillar, and the pillar to each supporting page, with link text that says what is on the other end. Add the author, the publication date and the sources list. Keep one clear page per question, and do not create near-copies for slightly different phrasings.

Step 8: Distribute

Posts and newsletters carry the argument to people who already follow you. Each one links to the page that holds the full answer. They are not new arguments. They are doors into the same argument.

Where n8n fits

We use n8n to remove the clerical work between steps. A Webhook node receives the notice that a new recording is ready. An HTTP Request node sends the file to the transcription service. A Code node cleans the transcript and splits it by time code. A Google Drive node files the results. A Wait node then holds the flow until a person has reviewed the drafts, and an IF node routes approved pieces onward and sends the rest back with comments. A Send Email node tells the team what needs attention.

The Wait node is the important one. Nothing is published until a person has approved it. We do not publish content that no person has read.

Step 9: Measure

After publishing, check three things each month: whether the pages are indexed, what Search Console reports for Google's generative features, and whether the engines cite you on your tracked buyer questions. We describe the method in how to measure AI citations. Judge the pipeline on those results, not on how many assets it produced.

How many assets is the right number?

As many as the source honestly supports, and no more. A long webinar with a strong argument and a rich Q&A may support a dozen or more distinct assets. A short one with a single point may support three. The number is what falls out of the extraction and the plan, not a quota to fill.

Common questions about turning a webinar into content

How long should the webinar be?

Length matters less than substance. A short webinar with a strong argument can support more useful assets than a long one with no position. Judge by how many distinct questions the speaker answers.

Is it safe to use AI to draft content from a transcript?

Yes, if a person checks it. Google says AI-generated content must be fact-checked before publishing, and that using AI to generate many pages without adding value can violate its scaled content abuse policy. Draft from the speaker's words and review every claim.

Should I publish a page for every question in the Q&A?

No. Group similar questions on one FAQ page and give separate pages only to questions that need a full answer. Multiple near-duplicate pages dilute your site and are the pattern Google warns about.

Do I need the speaker's approval?

Yes. The speaker should read any piece that carries their name or quotes them. It protects accuracy and the relationship.

Can I automate the whole pipeline?

You can automate transfers, transcription and filing. You should not automate approval. A person has to decide that the facts are right before anything goes live.

What if my webinar has no strong argument?

Then it will not make a strong content system. Use it for one or two pages, or choose a different source. A better recording is worth more than a longer plan.
