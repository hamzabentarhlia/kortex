Google calls this SEO, and says so plainly

The clearest public statement on the topic comes from Google itself. In its guide to optimizing for generative AI features, Google explains that these features are rooted in its core Search ranking and quality systems. It names two techniques they use. Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves relevant, current pages from the Search index and uses them to ground the answer. Query fan-out runs several related searches at once to gather more material.

On the labels, the guide is direct. "AEO" stands for answer engine optimization and "GEO" for generative engine optimization. Google says both describe work aimed at visibility in AI search experiences, and that from the point of view of Google Search, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and so still SEO.

That does not make the terms useless. A team that works on visibility across several engines needs a word for it. But it removes the idea that there is a separate, secret discipline for Google's AI answers. For those, the work is the work you should already be doing.

The technical bar is the same bar

To be shown as a supporting link in AI Overviews or AI Mode, Google says a page has to be indexed and eligible to be shown in Search with a snippet. It adds that there are no additional technical requirements. It also warns that meeting every requirement does not guarantee crawling, indexing or serving.

For a B2B site, that turns into a short list that any SEO audit would already contain:

  • Crawling is allowed in robots.txt and by any CDN or firewall in front of the site.
  • Important content is available as text, not only in images or video.
  • Pages are findable through internal links.
  • Structured data, where you use it, matches the visible text.

The firewall point deserves attention. Perplexity's documentation tells site owners who use a web application firewall that they may need to allow its bots explicitly. A firewall rule written to block scrapers can also block the crawler you want.

What changed: one question, many searches

A B2B buyer rarely asks one clean question. They ask something long: which approach to supplier risk works for a mid-size manufacturer with few suppliers in a few countries. Google's guide describes how its AI features handle this. The model generates several related queries, for example subtopics, and fetches results for each. Its own example turns a question about fixing a weedy lawn into searches about herbicides, chemical-free removal and prevention.

For a B2B company this changes the unit of competition. You are no longer fighting for one head term. You are trying to have a page that is good enough on one of the sub-questions that the system picks it up. A page that answers a narrow, specific question well has a real way in.

There is a trap here, and Google names it. Creating a separate page for every possible variation of a query, mainly to manipulate rankings or AI responses, violates its scaled content abuse policy. Google also says it is ineffective as a long-term strategy, because a large number of pages does not make a site more relevant. The answer is not more pages. It is better pages on questions your buyers actually have.

What changed: the content has to be worth retrieving

Google asks for non-commodity content. Its example of commodity content is a list of seven tips for first-time buyers: common knowledge that could come from anyone. Its example of non-commodity content is a first-hand account with a specific decision and a specific result.

In B2B this is the most useful distinction in the guide. Think of what a generic AI-written answer can already produce: definitions, frameworks everybody repeats, lists of best practices. A page that repeats them adds nothing for a retrieval system to prefer. What it cannot reproduce is your own data, your client's constraint, the thing you tried that did not work, and the reasoning that led to your recommendation.

This is where we start every project. The raw material is the expert's own call, webinar or talk, because that is where the first-hand material lives. The rest is structure.

What changed: measurement

Google says sites appearing in AI features are included in the overall search traffic in Search Console, and points to a Generative AI performance report there. That gives you a first-party view for Google.

It also warns against third-party tools that promise ranking success or claim to use internal Google metrics. No third-party tool has access to Google's internal ranking or AI systems. Use such tools if they help your workflow, the guide says, and judge their advice against the official guidance. We agree, and it is one reason we log citations ourselves, with the same prompts every month, instead of quoting a vendor score.

Other engines, other rules, less documentation

Google's guide covers Google. For other engines the public information is thinner. Perplexity documents two user agents. PerplexityBot is designed to surface and link websites in Perplexity search results, and is not used to crawl content for AI foundation models. Perplexity-User supports user actions: when a person asks a question, it may visit a page to answer and include a link. Because a user requested the fetch, Perplexity says this fetcher generally ignores robots.txt rules.

For ChatGPT and Gemini outside Google Search, we do not have primary documentation to quote on how sources are selected, so we do not state it as fact. What we can say is what is common to all of them: they need to reach the page, understand it and find something worth using.

What did not change

  • Pages must be crawlable, indexed and fast.
  • Helpful, reliable, people-first content beats content made for the system.
  • Internal links tell machines and people how your pages relate.
  • Trust matters most. Google's guidance on helpful content says that of experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness, trust is the most important.

What we tell B2B teams to do

  1. Run a normal technical SEO check first. Fix indexing and blocked crawlers before anything else.
  2. Pick the ten questions your buyers ask in sales calls. Write each answer with something only you know.
  3. Keep one clear page per question. Do not multiply pages for the same intent.
  4. Skip tactics Google lists as unnecessary for its Search: special markup, llms.txt files and chopping pages into tiny pieces.
  5. Track visibility with Search Console and a monthly manual check of the same prompts in each engine.

Where GEO advice is still unproven

Much of what circulates about GEO has no public evidence behind it. Claims about exact ideal page length, special phrasing, or prompt-matching formats are guesses unless the source shows its method. Google lists several of these as myths for its own Search, including chunking content, rewriting content only for AI systems and chasing inauthentic mentions. Where another engine publishes nothing, treat advice as a hypothesis, test it on your own pages and log the result.

Common questions about GEO and SEO

Is GEO different from SEO?

For Google Search, Google says optimizing for its generative AI features is still SEO, because those features rely on its core ranking and quality systems. The term GEO is useful for describing work across several engines, but it does not describe a separate set of rules for Google.

Do I need special markup to appear in AI Overviews?

No. Google says there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, and no special schema.org markup you need to add. A page has to be indexed and eligible to show a snippet.

Should I create a page for every variation of a question?

No. Google says doing so mainly to manipulate rankings or AI responses violates its scaled content abuse policy, and that a large number of pages does not make a site higher quality.

How do I measure visibility in Google's AI features?

Use the Generative AI performance report in Search Console. Google says AI feature traffic is included in your overall Search Console data. Third-party tools cannot see Google's internal metrics.

Does GEO work the same in ChatGPT and Perplexity?

We cannot say. Perplexity documents its crawlers, but no engine we looked at publishes a full description of how it picks sources. Test on your own pages and log the results instead of relying on a general rule.

Is hiring a GEO agency worth it?

Only if it can show its method and does not promise to game the system. Google advises evaluating third-party SEO advice against its official guidance. Ask what the agency will produce, how it checks facts and how it reports results.
