An entity is a name that points to one thing

An entity is a person, company, product, place or idea that a machine can identify without ambiguity. For a founder, three entities matter: the company, the person and the method or product name. Each one needs a single, stable description that the site, the profiles and the directories repeat.

Take an invented example. A supplier-risk software company calls itself "Acme Risk" on its site, "AcmeRisk Ltd" on LinkedIn and "Acme" in a directory listing. Its founder is "J. Moreau" in bylines and "Julie Moreau-Rossi" on conference pages. A person reading all of this knows it is one company and one founder. A system that has to decide whether to quote a page from "Acme Risk" and a page from "AcmeRisk Ltd" together has to guess. This is an Example, not a real company.

The cost of the guess is not dramatic. It is a reason to hesitate, and an engine that hesitates can pick a source it identifies more easily.

Google asks who wrote it, and wants the honest answer

Google's guidance on helpful content suggests evaluating content by "Who, How and Why". The "Who" part is concrete: is it self-evident to visitors who authored the content, do pages carry a byline where one is expected, and do bylines lead to further information about the author, with background on the areas they write about.

The same page adds a warning. Google says to avoid deceptive authorship information, and describes fabricated creator profiles, such as AI-generated headshots, made-up names or false credentials, as a form of deception that makes a page untrustworthy to users and to its automated quality systems.

For a founder this settles a common temptation. You do not need an impressive author page. You need a true one: your real name, what you do, what you have done that is relevant, and a way to check it. Our own about page follows that rule, and it names the pipeline we built and the market we worked in because those are the parts that can be verified.

A true, modest author page beats an impressive one that cannot be checked.

Four places to say the same thing

Most entity problems come from the same four places. Fix them in this order, because each one depends on the one before.

Where a founder states the entity, and what to check
PlaceWhat it statesWhat to check
About pageWho you are, where you are based, what the company doesCompany name spelled exactly as everywhere else; your full name
Author bylineWho wrote each articleLinks to an author page; same name form on every article
Organization and Person markupName, URL, logo, profiles that are yoursEvery value also appears as visible text on the site
Profiles you controlLinkedIn, speaker pages, directoriesSame company name, same description, a link back to the site

On markup, Google's documentation for Organization data lists the properties it uses to understand a company: name, url, logo, sameAs for profiles on other sites, and contact details. The sameAs list is where the founder's discipline shows. List only profiles that are really yours, and make sure each one says the same thing the site says. Our article on schema markup covers which types are worth adding and why the markup has to match the visible text.

Should the site lead with the founder or the company?

Lead with whichever buyers already know. A founder with a known name and a company nobody has heard of should put the name first and link it to the company. A company with a known brand should put the brand first and link it to the people. The rule that matters is consistency: pick the order, then use it in every byline, bio and profile.

A ten-minute entity audit

You can find most of the gaps without a tool. Open a private window and run these checks, writing each result down.

  1. Search your company name in quotes. Note every spelling that appears. Anything that differs from the form on your site is a task.
  2. Search your full name with the company. Check that the first results are profiles and pages you control or can edit.
  3. Search your method or product name. If a competitor, a dictionary term or a different spelling dominates, decide whether to rename or to define the term on a glossary page of your own.
  4. Open your last five articles. Each should show a byline that links to an author page, and a publication date and last-updated date.
  5. View the source of your home page. Find the Organization markup. Compare every value with what a visitor can read on the page.
  6. Check three profiles. LinkedIn, one directory, one speaker page. Same company name, same one-line description, a link to the site.

This is the same family of checks as the entity and trust group in the GEO Audit Checklist, which lists six of its 27 checks under that heading. Doing them yourself first makes any later audit cheaper, because the gaps that remain are the interesting ones.

A worked example of the fix

Back to the invented company. The founder picks "Acme Risk" as the one spelling, because it is what customers say. She updates the LinkedIn page and the directory listing. She picks "Julie Moreau" as the byline form and adds a short author page that links to her speaker page and to the company. The Organization markup gets the same name, the logo and two profile URLs that really belong to the company. She adds a glossary entry for her method, "supplier risk scoring", so the term has one definition on her own site.

None of this is clever. It removes the reasons for hesitation. In the next monthly citation check she records whether the pages she cares about are cited, using the log from our measurement article, and waits for three runs before reading any change.

What this does not do

Clear entities do not guarantee a citation. Google states that there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode beyond the same SEO fundamentals, and that no special markup is needed. For other engines we found no public statement either way. Entity work is hygiene that supports everything else, and it is cheap enough to do whether or not it moves a number.

Common questions about entity SEO for founders

What is entity SEO in plain terms?

It is the work of making sure a machine can tell who or what you are without guessing: one spelling of the company name, a real author for each article, markup that matches the visible text, and profiles that say the same thing.

Do I need a Wikipedia page or a knowledge panel?

No. Neither is required and neither can be created on demand. Start with the site and the profiles you control, and keep them consistent.

Is Person markup worth adding for the founder?

It is cheap and harmless when it matches a visible author page. Google does not say it is required for its AI features, so treat it as a clear statement of who you are, not as a tactic.

What if my name or company name is shared with someone else?

Add qualifiers you can keep stable, such as the company name next to your name and the city on the about page, and link the profiles that are really yours from the site so the connection is explicit.

Should I use a pen name or an unnamed team?

Google asks for accurate authorship information. A team byline is fine when it is true and links to a page about the team. Invented personas are the part to avoid.

How long until I see a result?

Engines recrawl on their own schedule and nobody publishes one. Log your citations monthly and wait for three runs before judging a change.
