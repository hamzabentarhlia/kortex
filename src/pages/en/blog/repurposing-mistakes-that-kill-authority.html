Key takeaways
  • The test for every derived asset is simple: does it answer a question the source did not answer in this form, and does it keep the proof?
  • Google lists generating many pages without adding value, and automated translation done to scrape, as examples of scaled content abuse.

Repurposing is the easy way to lose what you had

The logic of repurposing is sound. One source holds more questions than one page can answer, and buyers meet your expertise in different formats. A 50-minute webinar contains a pillar article, a set of FAQ answers, a comparison and a dozen posts. We build exactly this at KORTEX, and the method is on how it works.

The same logic, applied carelessly, produces the opposite result: ten pieces that say the same thing less well than the original. Each mistake below comes with what it looks like, why it costs you, and the fix.

1. The same text on several URLs

A consultancy publishes its article, then a "short version" on a partner blog and a full copy in a resource library, all with the same paragraphs. Google's documentation on duplicate URLs explains that a canonical preference consolidates signals for similar or duplicate pages and saves crawling time. The strongest method it lists is a redirect, followed by a rel="canonical" link annotation, which it calls a strong signal. If a copy has to exist, point it at the original. If it does not need to exist, remove it.

2. One idea split into thin pages

Query fan-out means one buyer question can trigger several related searches, so pages that answer real sub-questions have more ways in. The temptation is to invent sub-questions. A page that rephrases its neighbour adds a URL and no information. Google names "using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users" as an example of scaled content abuse, and "stitching or combining content from different web pages without adding value" as another. Write one page for each distinct question the source answers, and merge the rest into the pillar.

Ten pages that say the same thing are one page and nine liabilities.

3. Mechanical translation

Translating an article into French and Arabic widens the audience. Publishing the raw output of a translation tool does the opposite. Google lists "automated transformations like synonymizing, translating, or other obfuscation techniques" among the ways scaled content is generated when little value is provided to users. Localized versions are not considered duplicates when the main content is actually translated, but a poor translation is its own problem. The method we use for French and Arabic is in GEO in French and Arabic.

4. Dropping the proof

A webinar slide says that payment delays show up months before a missed delivery, with the figure and the case behind it. The LinkedIn post keeps the sentence and loses the figure. A reader, or an engine, now sees a claim with nothing to check. The fix is a rule for the pipeline: every derived asset that repeats a claim also carries the number, the example or a link to the page that has them. At KORTEX every asset keeps a pointer back to where the claim appears in your source, so an editor can check it.

What "keep the proof" looks like in practice

Take a claim extracted from a webinar at minute 18: a figure and the example behind it. The pillar article states both. The FAQ answer states the claim and links to the pillar. The post states the claim, the figure and a link. The editor checks all three against the recording. Nothing is repeated without its evidence nearby.

5. No byline and no date

Google asks "Who, How and Why" of content, and suggests bylines that lead to background about the author. A derived asset that appears with no author and no date looks like what scaled content looks like. Name the author, show both the publication date and the last-updated date, and where automation was used, say how. Google encourages sharing how a piece was created when readers might wonder.

6. Assets that do not link to each other

Google's AI features guidance lists making content easily findable through internal links among the fundamentals that continue to matter. Repurposed assets are the easiest content to link, because you know exactly how they relate. The failure is leaving it for later. Build the linking map when the assets are built: the pillar links to each question page, each question page links back to the pillar and to its nearest sibling, and every post links to the page it summarizes.

7. Never going back

A cluster published once decays as a unit. A figure that was right in 2024 is wrong in 2026, and it sits on five pages. Give every asset a review date when it ships and a reason to revisit it: a changed price, a new Google announcement, a customer question that was not answered. When you update, change the visible date and the content in the same edit. A date that moves with no change behind it is worse than an old date.

A short test before anything ships

  1. Does this asset answer a question the source did not answer in this form?
  2. Does it carry the number, example or link that supports its claim?
  3. Is there a named author, a date and a way to reach the source?
  4. Does it link to at least two sibling assets, and do they link back?
  5. Is there exactly one canonical version of the text?

Common questions about repurposing content

Is repurposing content duplicate content?

Not when each asset answers a distinct question in its own words. It becomes a problem when identical text sits on several URLs. In that case choose one canonical page and point the others to it.

How many assets can one source support?

As many as it has distinct questions, claims and examples. A strong 50-minute source often supports 20 to 24 assets in a SWARM month. A thin one supports fewer, and we say so at intake.

Can I post a blog article on LinkedIn as is?

You can, but the platform copy will not carry your canonical signals. Post a shorter version with the claim, the proof and a link to the article instead.

Does Google penalize AI-assisted repurposing?

Google's policy targets generating many pages without adding value, however they are produced. Its guidance asks for manual fact-checking and review of AI-generated content before publishing.

How often should repurposed assets be reviewed?

Set a review date when each asset ships. Quarterly is a sensible default for anything with figures or prices, and sooner when the source facts change.

Should translated pages be separate URLs?

Yes. Google recommends a separate URL for each language version, with hreflang annotations linking them.
