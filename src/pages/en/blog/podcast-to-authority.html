Audio is a closed box for most readers and engines

A good episode can hold an hour of specific advice. A buyer who asks an assistant a long question will not hear it, because the assistant works from text it can retrieve. Google's guidance on AI features lists, among the fundamentals that still apply, "making sure that important content is available in textual form". For a podcaster that sentence is a to-do list: the knowledge exists, and it has to be written down where it can be found.

This is the same reasoning behind our podcast service page. One episode becomes a month of answers when the text is built with care, and a few quick posts when it is not.

A transcript is not an article

Pasting a transcript on a page makes the content searchable and does little else. It is long, it wanders, it repeats, and it has none of the structure a reader or a retrieval system needs to find the answer to one question. Google's description of how raters assess main content separates high effort, such as original analysis, from low effort, such as using generative AI to produce large amounts of text without manual oversight or curation. A raw transcript sits near the second end.

Treat the transcript as the source for extraction. The article, the question pages and the posts are built from it, and each one is a decision about what to keep.

A transcript tells you what was said. An article decides what was worth saying.

Choosing the episode

Not every episode converts well. The ones that do share four traits.

  1. One main topic, explored in depth. A survey of five topics yields five thin pages.
  2. Practical advice, not only stories. Stories help, but readers searching for an answer want the steps.
  3. Thirty minutes or more. Shorter episodes rarely hold enough distinct questions.
  4. Clear audio we can transcribe. A poor recording introduces errors before review begins.

Solo episodes and expert interviews work best. Casual conversations can work if they contain clear advice, which is something to settle at intake rather than after the work.

What we pull out of an episode

Extraction produces a list, with a pointer to the minute where each item appears. Five categories are worth the effort.

  • The strongest opinions of host and guest. These are what no other site has.
  • Questions asked and how they were answered. Each becomes a candidate question page.
  • Frameworks, lists and step-by-step advice, which become how-to sections.
  • Stories that illustrate a point. They give an article first-hand material, the quality Google's helpful-content questions ask about when they mention first-hand expertise.
  • Names, tools and resources mentioned, which need checking for spelling and accuracy before they appear anywhere.

A worked example, invented for illustration

This is an Example, not a client result. A business coach publishes a 50-minute interview about how solo consultants should price an engagement. The extraction list has eleven items: three strong opinions, four questions the host asked, two frameworks and two stories. The episode article is built around the main opinion, that day rates reward slowness. Four of the questions become pages: how to move from day rates to project pricing, when to raise a price, how to say no to a discount and how to quote scope. The frameworks become two glossary entries. The stories supply the examples in the article. The posts each carry one opinion with a link back.

When the guest says something you do not agree with

The article reports what the guest said as theirs, attributed by name. It does not adopt it as the host's position. If the host wants to respond, that response is a separate, labeled paragraph taken from the recording or written with the host's approval. An editor checks that every attributed statement matches the audio.

Handling the guest

A guest's words are not yours to reshape. Three habits keep the work honest. Attribute each statement to the speaker by name. Check every figure, tool name and company name against the recording. Send the guest the draft quotes before publishing when you have the relationship to do so, because they are the one person who can catch a misreading.

Measuring what happened

Write down ten questions a buyer might ask about the episode's topic before you publish. Run them monthly in the engines your buyers use, log who is cited, and read the trend over at least three runs. The method is in how to measure AI citations without a tool budget. A podcast page can earn a citation without earning one for months, and a missing citation is information, not a verdict.

Common questions about turning podcasts into content

Is a podcast transcript enough to rank or be cited?

It makes the words available as text, which is a start. A structured article and question pages that answer specific questions give a reader or an engine something to quote.

How long should the episode be?

Thirty minutes or more. Shorter episodes rarely contain enough separate questions to support several pages.

Can you use an episode with a guest?

Yes, with the guest's statements attributed to them and checked against the recording.

Do I need the original audio file?

We need audio that can be transcribed accurately. A published episode link or the file both work, and clear sound matters more than the format.

Will the episode articles sound like me?

They are built from your transcript, keep your phrases and examples, and are checked against your voice and terminology guide before you approve them.

How soon should I expect citations?

Nobody can say. Engines work on their own schedules. Log a fixed list of questions monthly and judge the trend over three runs.
